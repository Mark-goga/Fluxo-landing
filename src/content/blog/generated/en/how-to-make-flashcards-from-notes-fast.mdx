## Active Recall Beats Rereading

Testing yourself on material, rather than reading it again, ranks among the strongest study methods. It works best on facts with no inherent logic: vocabulary, terminology, dates, formulas. Rereading feels productive because the words look familiar, but familiarity is not retrieval, and only retrieval builds the pathway you need during an exam.

Your brain runs as a network of connections, and the ones you use often get stronger. Each time you pull a definition out of memory instead of reading it, you travel that path again and harden it. That is the whole mechanism behind repeated recall practice.

The usual way to get active recall is flashcards, which means rewriting everything you already wrote once. The duplicate-and-erase method gives you the same retrieval practice from the notes you already have.

## The Duplicate-and-Erase Method

Open your notes in an app that can duplicate a page, then duplicate the page you want to study. You now have two copies of the same material, and each one has a job. The first stays untouched as your reference version.

On the second copy, your recall version, take the digital eraser to whatever you need to practice retrieving. That could be definitions, vocabulary terms, dates, formula names, process steps, tables, or graphs. Erase the answer, not the whole area around it.

Always leave a cue visible, because a blank page prompts nothing. For vocabulary, erase the definition and keep the word. For formulas, erase the name and leave the equation. Dates work the same way: the year goes, the event stays, and the event is what pulls the year back out of memory.

Once you have filled the blanks from memory, open the reference copy and compare. Underline or highlight everything you got wrong or half-remembered. Then run the same loop on those items: duplicate, erase less this time so more context stays visible, and check yourself again.

## Why This Works Better Than Flashcards

Flashcards and duplicate-and-erase both give you retrieval practice, so the difference is workflow. Making flashcards means copying information out of your notes into a second format, which is the same content written twice. Erasing works on the notes you already wrote.

Flexibility is the bigger win. Testing a concept a different way with flashcards means building another card, while here you only change how much you erase. Wipe the whole definition to test meaning, then keep the definition and erase letters from the word to test spelling. One note, two tests, no new cards.

## No Tablet? Use Paper and Pencil

Without a tablet, write your notes in pencil or erasable pen and photograph or scan the page first. That image becomes your fixed reference copy, which matters because you are about to destroy parts of the original. Then erase sections of the handwritten page to build your recall version.

Paper has a limit that screens do not: friction damage. Sturdy paper survives roughly three to five erasures on the same spot before the fibers fray and tear through. Plan your rounds around that number, or shoot a fresh reference photo when you want more cycles than the page can take.

## Works Across All Study Materials

This method fits anywhere active recall helps: foreign language vocabulary, terminology in any subject, formulas and their names, steps in a procedure, tables comparing categories, graphs showing relationships. The best fit is material where the answer is narrow, meaning one word, one date, one formula name. Broad essay-style questions do not erase cleanly.

The rule stays the same across all of it. Keep enough context visible to prompt memory, erase the precise piece you need to retrieve, and let your brain do the pulling.
