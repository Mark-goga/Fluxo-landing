## How this comparison was put together

Five tools, four criteria: how well spaced repetition actually works, how fast you can start, whether cards connect to your notes, and how the whole thing fits a self-directed learner. Anki carries the retention argument, Quizlet carries the low-friction one, RemNote and Mochi sit in the notes-first camp, and Fluxo generates cards from notes you already wrote. Every claim below traces to published documentation and hands-on feature review.

## Why self-learners get stuck between ease and retention

A software engineer learning Rust, a resident cramming for boards, a language learner with a deadline: all three write dense, structured notes built from reading and practice. Then they hit the same wall. The flashcard tools that remember well are slow to feed, and the ones that are fast to feed forget.

Anki gives you scheduling that maximizes long-term retention, and it works: study consistently and you will still know the material a year out. The price is that you convert every concept into a card by hand. One chapter of dense material can eat an evening, and that's before you count the time spent learning Anki itself.

Quizlet inverts the deal. Paste a list, grab a set someone else built, start reviewing in minutes. But its review scheduling isn't rigorous spaced repetition, so what you gain in speed you give back in retention a few months later.

RemNote and Mochi tried to split the difference by binding cards to notes. Neither added AI-driven card generation, so you still build every card by hand. You just stay inside your notes app while you do it.

None of them answer the obvious question: if you already wrote structured notes about the material, why rebuild those same notes as flashcards? That's duplicated work with no payoff. What a self-learner actually needs is a tool that reads the notes, generates the review cards, schedules them properly, and keeps them in step when the notes change.

## Anki: the spaced repetition standard

Anki's algorithm schedules each card's next review based on how well you answered it. Cards you fumble come back soon; cards you nail go quiet for weeks or months. Run that over months and years and retention lands far above anything passive review produces, with less total study time.

Card customization has no ceiling. Text, images, audio, and video can all live on a single card. Language learners drop in native-speaker recordings; medical students embed anatomical diagrams.

Progress syncs across devices, so a session on your desktop carries to your phone. Anki is open-source and continuously developed by community volunteers, and add-ons cover most of what the core app doesn't: advanced statistics packages plot your retention curves, custom card types open up study formats the defaults never anticipated.

### Anki's friction points

The learning curve is the real cost. Decks, note types, fields: the vocabulary assumes you already know the system. Someone who expects to open an app and study within a minute instead meets a setup phase, and getting sync configured and a deck structure that holds up takes hours rather than minutes.

Interactivity stays thin. Anki flips cards and tracks retention, and that's the whole experience. There's no Match, no Gravity, no timed drill, so learners who need variety to stay engaged find it monotonous.

Then there's the authoring load. Every card you review is one you wrote, which is fine for a hundred cards and brutal for a thousand.

## Quizlet: frictionless, but compromised on memory

Nothing about Quizlet slows you down. Create a set, type terms and definitions, start studying, all inside a few minutes with no prior knowledge assumed. That's why it owns classrooms and why most students reach for it by reflex.

The study modes fight repetition fatigue. Flashcards handle core review, practice tests handle self-assessment, and Match and Gravity turn drilling into something closer to a game. Learners who need variety stay with Quizlet longer than they'd last with plain card-flipping.

Sharing is built in, so a cohort preparing for the same exam can all study from one set. The mobile app works offline, which covers the flight or the subway, and cards support images and audio the way Anki's do.

### Quizlet's retention problem

Quizlet doesn't apply spaced repetition as effectively as Anki. Its review scheduling is simplified, and simplified scheduling means weaker long-term retention. If your target is remembering this material six months from now, that gap works against you the whole time, and it compounds across years of study.

The tradeoff is honest enough: Quizlet picked ease over retention science. For a vocabulary quiz on Friday or high-school material you'll never touch again, that's the right call. For professional certification or coursework that has to stick, you pay for the shortcut later.

## RemNote: notes and flashcards in one place

RemNote targets students who want notes and flashcards in the same place, with card generation following from how the notes are structured. That matches how a lot of people already study. What's missing is depth: detailed feature comparisons and retention data aren't publicly available enough to support a strong verdict here.

If you keep structured notes and want flashcards to derive from them, RemNote is worth a look on your own material. Just go in knowing the evidence base for ranking it against Quizlet is thin.

## Mochi: markdown flashcards with clean design

Mochi keeps flashcards markdown-native inside a minimalist interface, which feels natural if you already write in markdown and want nothing heavier. The design is genuinely uncluttered. As with RemNote, though, independent verification of its spaced-repetition quality and long-term retention outcomes is sparse, so the tool has users and a clear point of view but not much published evidence behind it as a Quizlet replacement.

## Fluxo: retention without the manual grind

Fluxo is built for people who write their own notes and want the review cards to come from them. You organize material into spaces and topics, write rich-text notes, and the AI generates flashcards, quizzes, and summaries from what you wrote. The authoring step you'd spend an evening on in Anki disappears into the notes you were writing anyway.

Scheduling follows spaced repetition, so material resurfaces on a rhythm built for retention instead of convenience. Your notes and cards stay connected: update a note and the cards reflect the change, so you're not maintaining two versions of the same knowledge.

A companion mascot, streaks, and gamified review sessions handle the motivation side, and the AI suggests new topics to explore based on what you've been studying. That last part matters more than it sounds, because it surfaces connections across subjects you were treating separately.

## Where Fluxo doesn't fit your needs

There's no shared-deck library. You can't browse and download sets other learners built the way you can on Quizlet, because every card comes from notes you wrote yourself. Wanting to study a published curriculum without writing anything first rules Fluxo out.

Classroom and instructor tools are absent too. No assigning decks to students, no progress dashboards, no cohort management, so a teacher shopping for a class platform should look elsewhere.

The AI generates cards from notes you provide, and it won't write the notes for you. The whole design assumes that putting the material into your own words is part of how you learn it, which makes "generate my notes from scratch" a request the product deliberately doesn't answer.

Card customization also stays tighter than Anki's: no arbitrary audio, video, or interactive elements dropped into individual cards. That limit buys coherence, since cards inherit their structure from your notes and stay aligned with them. Anki optimizes for unlimited customization, Fluxo for keeping notes and cards in sync.

## How to choose based on your learning style

**Choose Anki if** you want the most proven scheduling algorithm and you'll accept setup complexity plus manual card creation to get it. It rewards patience and high retention demands.

**Choose Quizlet if** ease and sharing matter more than what you'll remember next year, or you need classmates studying from the same set. Fastest onboarding of the five.

**Choose RemNote or Mochi if** you already keep structured notes in a dedicated tool and want a light flashcard layer without switching apps. Test retention on your own material before you commit, since published evidence is limited.

**Choose Fluxo if** you write structured notes and want cards generated from them automatically, on a spaced-repetition schedule, with almost no setup. Your notes stay the primary artifact and the cards follow.

## Getting cards out of notes you already wrote

Right now the gap between reading a chapter and reviewing it is an evening of typing. You finish the material, then retype it as question-and-answer pairs, and by card sixty you're copying mechanically rather than thinking. Skip that step and the notes just sit there, unreviewed, fading on schedule.

Picture the same chapter where finishing the notes is finishing the work. Cards, a quiz, and a summary already exist, the first review lands tomorrow, and the rest arrive spaced out over the weeks when you'd otherwise have forgotten.

That's the whole of what Fluxo does: spaces and topics for your notes, AI-generated flashcards, quizzes, and summaries built from them, spaced-repetition scheduling that brings material back over time, and a mascot with streaks to keep the sessions happening. It won't hand you a ready-made deck or a course, because there's no shared library and no AI writing notes on your behalf. You write, it schedules.
