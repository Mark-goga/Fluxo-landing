Picking the best flashcard app comes down to one decision: how much of your study time goes into making cards versus actually reviewing them? Every app here runs on spaced repetition, scheduling reviews at intervals that fight forgetting. Where they split hard is the work you do before the first review prompt ever appears. That gap, between card-making overhead and review time, is where most learners stall, and where these five apps separate.

## How We Compared

The benchmarks here come from documented hands-on use of Anki by a language learner who built roughly 2,700 words and phrases across English and Hebrew through spaced repetition. That case sets the concrete numbers for card creation time and daily prep burden. From there, each app gets measured on the same axes: how much setup it demands, what the review mechanic trains, whether AI assistance exists and what it actually does, and which workflows it fits.

Pricing for RemNote, Mochi, and Quizlet could not be verified from available sources, so the table marks it accordingly. Anki's iPhone price reflects the date of research and may have changed since.

## Why Spaced Repetition Determines Long-Term Retention

Every app here rests on the same mechanism. Memory decays on a predictable curve, and reviewing material right before it fades reinforces the trace far more efficiently than grinding through everything daily. One learner's documented run to roughly 2,700 words shows what steady spaced repetition produces over sustained practice.

For vocabulary, card design matters as much as scheduling. A card showing a target word next to its bare translation trains isolated recognition, which is a poor match for the recall you need when that word turns up mid-sentence in reading or speech. Hide the word inside an example sentence instead, and the card trains contextual recall that transfers to real use. That single design choice splits these five apps as much as any price or platform difference.

## Anki: Unmatched Scheduling, Maximum Setup Cost

Anki is the benchmark in spaced repetition software. Its scheduling algorithm has been sharpened over years of active development, the community is huge, and the add-on ecosystem reaches specialized cases no other app bothers with. For learners with specific needs and patience for configuration, nothing else here touches its scheduling depth.

### Why Setup Takes More Time Than Expected

Anki's settings expose parameters for how aggressively new cards enter rotation, how fast review intervals grow, and how the algorithm handles missed reviews. These interact with each other, and the defaults suit no single learner or subject perfectly. New users routinely burn hours on external tutorials before they trust their configuration, and that friction returns for veterans whenever they switch subjects or study habits.

### Card Creation Is the Main Time Tax

Building one vocabulary card with a definition, an example sentence, and an image means copying each field by hand from an outside source. That runs 2-3 minutes per card. Want 20 new words a day? You spend 40-60 minutes preparing before a single review. For plenty of Anki users, card creation becomes the dominant daily activity, which flips the tool's whole purpose.

### Mobile Limitations and Pricing

Desktop Anki plays pronunciation audio through a third-party add-on. That add-on will not install on a phone, so mobile users study without sound unless they recorded the audio through some other process first.

At the time of research, the official Anki iPhone app cost $25 as a one-time purchase. The desktop version and the Android app are free.

## RemNote: Notes-First, Curriculum-Dependent

RemNote is built for students who already keep their material in hierarchical notes. You annotate your own outlines, and the app turns marked sections into flashcards. It suits medical students, law students, and anyone on a structured syllabus who wants note-taking and spaced review in one environment.

The catch is the assumption baked in: your material has to arrive in a shape that maps to a hierarchy. Vocabulary picked up during language immersion, insights gathered across scattered reading, or knowledge earned through experience resists that clean outline. RemNote shines when you work from a structured external resource and stumbles when the source is your own accumulated experience. Pricing and platform details are not verified here.

## Mochi: Markdown-Native Cards With a Minimal Review Experience

Mochi targets learners who live in Markdown and want a clean split between making cards and reviewing them. The editor stays close to plain text, the review screen is deliberately bare, and there is no AI, no social layer, no gamification. For anyone who finds other apps cluttered, or whose workflow already runs on Markdown, that restraint is a genuine advantage.

The gap shows the moment you want more from a flashcard app. Mochi has no AI-generated cards, no shared library, and no structured review loop with progress feedback. Learners after image-rich cards, a gamified session format, or a more engaging mobile experience will read its minimalism as a limit rather than a feature. Pricing and platform details are not verified here.

## Quizlet: Social Content and Speed, Shallow Long-Term Scheduling

Quizlet grew on shared study sets. Millions of pre-made decks span high school and university subjects across most disciplines, making it the fastest way to start reviewing for a specific exam when someone else already built the cards. For short-deadline prep on structured content, that shared library erases the card creation step entirely.

Its review games aim at short-term recall activation, not long-term retention. The real draw is social: finding decks other people made, sharing your own, moving through game modes that keep sessions quick and engaging. Spaced repetition sits in the feature set, but learners chasing durable knowledge over months keep finding its scheduling depth thin next to dedicated SRS tools. Pricing is not verified here.

## Fluxo: Write Your Notes, Let AI Handle the Cards

Fluxo shifts where AI steps into the study cycle. You write rich-text notes inside spaces and topics you organize yourself, and Fluxo generates flashcards, quizzes, and summaries from them. The AI reads what you wrote and builds the review layer on top, instead of writing your notes for you or pulling content from outside sources. The notes stay yours; the AI works from them.

### The Review Loop

Once cards come out of your notes, Fluxo's spaced repetition scheduler resurfaces them over time using the same spacing logic behind long-term retention. Gamified sessions, a companion mascot, and streaks give the practice loop a shape that helps many self-directed learners hold consistency without outside accountability. Fluxo also offers AI suggestions for new topics to study next, which answers a recurring stall for independent learners with no preset curriculum to follow.

### What the AI Does and Does Not Do

Fluxo's AI generates flashcards, quizzes, and summaries from notes you have already written. Hand it a topic name and it will not conjure the notes for you, and it will not browse external sources or process uploaded documents on your behalf. Your writing is the input; study materials are the output. Learners who want to build a system from their own understanding of a subject, rather than someone else's cards or a platform's curriculum, fit this model naturally.

## Where Fluxo Falls Short

Fluxo has no library of pre-made decks. If you need to start reviewing for an exam today on cards someone else created, Quizlet gets you there faster. Fluxo starts with you writing your own notes, and that prerequisite adds time before the first review session, which makes it a weak fit for last-minute exam prep.

There is no shared content marketplace. You cannot download community card sets the way AnkiWeb allows, and you cannot search decks by subject the way Quizlet's library does. Every space in Fluxo begins with your own writing, a strength for long-term original study and a limit for prepackaged course content.

Fluxo is a personal learning tool with no classroom or teacher management features. It cannot assign material to a group, track a class's progress, or run curriculum delivery for many students. Educators who need those capabilities will want a different platform.

The AI generates study materials from your notes, not from raw documents, audio recordings, or external URLs. If your source material sits in a PDF or a recorded lecture, you write notes from it first before Fluxo can generate anything.

## Which App Fits Your Learning Workflow

Built Anki decks over years and care most about scheduling reliability? Anki stays the strongest pick here. The configuration overhead is mostly a one-time cost, and the algorithm's long-term retention beats most alternatives.

If you learn by writing out your own understanding of a subject, Fluxo matches that workflow. You write notes on what you study, and the AI produces flashcards, quizzes, and summaries that test that exact understanding. The spaced repetition scheduler takes it from there, and the gamified loop keeps sessions consistent. This fits self-directed learners, researchers, language students, and anyone who already takes notes as part of their process and wants AI to turn them into a full review system.

For fast exam prep on existing content, Quizlet's shared library drops card creation entirely. For students in structured academic programs who want note-taking and review in one tool, RemNote's outline approach consolidates the process. For learners who prefer a keyboard-native, distraction-free card editor with no extra layers, Mochi delivers exactly that.
