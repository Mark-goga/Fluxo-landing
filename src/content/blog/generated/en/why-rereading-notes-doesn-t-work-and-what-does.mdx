Rereading your notes feels like studying, but it builds weak long-term memory. In Roediger and Karpicke's 2006 experiment, students who reread a passage scored higher on a test five minutes later, then fell behind at two days and again at one week. The method that holds up is retrieval, which means pulling information out of memory instead of pouring it back in.

## Where the evidence on rereading comes from

In 2013, a review led by John Dunlosky evaluated 10 learning techniques, including highlighting, rereading, summarizing, self-explanation, and practice testing, and rated how useful each one was across different students and materials. Two experiments carry most of the case against rereading. Roediger and Karpicke (2006) compared rereading with recall practice, while Karpicke and Blunt (2011), published in Science, tested retrieval practice against building concept maps.

## Why rereading feels productive but isn't

### Fluency makes familiar text feel learned

A page gets easier to read each time you go over it, and your brain mistakes that smoothness for learning. Researchers call the feeling fluency. The trouble is that a page can flow perfectly while you still could not write its key points from memory.

### Recognition differs from recall

Spotting material on a page and producing it from memory are two different skills. Exams ask for production, because you have to write, solve, or explain with the page out of sight. Rereading trains recognition, so the practice you put in doesn't match the test you will actually face.

### Rereading hides gaps

With the text in view, every sentence looks familiar, and you never find out what you can't retrieve. Highlighting has the same blind spot, since the material never leaves the page in front of you. Dunlosky's review rated both highlighting and rereading as low utility, even though they are among the most common study habits.

## What the experiments show about retention

### Short-term test scores mislead

In the 2006 Roediger and Karpicke experiment, the rereaders won on a test given five minutes after study. By two days the result had reversed, and at one week the group that practiced recall was still ahead. A quick self-check right after studying tells you very little about what you will remember later.

### Retrieval outperforms note-based strategies

Karpicke and Blunt (2011) found that retrieval practice beat building concept maps. Across the wider research, retrieval also outperformed restudying, note-taking, and other elaborative strategies. If you study from notes, take this seriously, because note-taking and concept mapping both feel productive and both ranked below plain recall.

### Meta-analyses confirm a moderate benefit

A 2014 meta-analysis pooled 159 comparisons and found a reliable, moderate benefit of retrieval practice over restudying. A 2021 review of 50 classroom experiments found medium or large benefits in most of them. Those classroom numbers suggest the effect holds up outside the lab, though results still vary by material.

## How the testing effect works

The testing effect is the finding that retrieving information improves long-term retention more than spending the same time restudying it. You will also see it called retrieval practice, practice testing, active recall, or test-enhanced learning. It is distinct from the broader practice effect.

### Direct and mediated effects

Researchers separate two routes by which testing helps. The direct effect is that the memory itself gets stronger each time you retrieve it. The mediated effect works through your behavior: a test shows you which items you don't know, so you spend your next session on those items.

### Why failed recall still helps

A failed recall attempt marks a gap in your understanding or memory. That miss tells you where to look when you go back to the material, so it works as a study instruction rather than a setback. Rereading tends to hide these gaps, while retrieval puts them right in front of you.

## What works better: practice testing and distributed practice

Practice testing means trying to pull information out of memory rather than putting it in again. In concrete terms, you close the book, attempt the answer, and only then check it. Dunlosky's review ranked it among the most useful of the 10 techniques it evaluated.

Distributed practice means spreading study over days instead of cramming into one night, and the same review rated it alongside practice testing as a top method. The two combine well. Instead of one long block, run short recall sessions spread across several days.

## A three-step recall routine for your notes

Run this on any set of notes you need to remember. It turns the research above into something you can do at your desk tonight with nothing but a blank page.

1. Read the material once.
2. Close the notes and write down everything you remember on a blank page.
3. Check your notes, then mark what you missed.

Your marked gaps are now your study list, so repeat recall on those gaps only and come back to them on another day. Expect the attempts to feel hard, because that discomfort signals the method is working. Check your notes right after each attempt, since retrieval works best when you get feedback on what you got wrong.

### Other ways to practice retrieval

Writing on a blank page is one format, and you can mix in others once the habit sticks. Retrieval practice also works through:

- Flashcards, answering before flipping
- Practice questions and past papers
- Explaining a topic aloud without notes

## Limits of the evidence and when to reread

Retrieval works best when you can check your answers. Without a way to check, you have no idea whether your recall was accurate, which is why the third step of the routine matters as much as the first two.

If you know almost nothing about a topic yet, read first. Rereading is fine as a first pass, but it shouldn't become your main method. Build a basic picture of the material, then shift to recall as soon as you have something to recall.

Much of the evidence comes from lab and classroom studies with specific materials, so results vary between learners and subjects, and no grade is guaranteed. Treat retrieval practice as the strongest option the research supports, then judge it by how much more you can recall after a few weeks of using it.
