Premade flashcard decks and AI flashcards built from your own notes split on one question that matters more than any feature list: who decides what goes on each card. A premade deck makes that call for you, which means you skip the work of choosing what matters and how ideas connect. The comparison below uses Anki as the reference point, then lines up Fluxo, Anki, RemNote, Mochi, and Quizlet so you can match the tool to your subject.

The case for writing your own cards rests on one long-time Anki user's experience with premade decks and on a basic learning principle: assembling information is a large part of learning it. The case for premade decks is narrower. It holds mainly for subjects that are pure memorization, and even there only when the deck makes you connect ideas.

## Where these claims come from

The core arguments here come from one long-time Anki user's reasoning about premade decks, not from controlled measurements. Any personal numbers describe one person's routine, so treat them as an illustration rather than a benchmark you should expect to hit.

The criticisms of premade decks, the card-making argument, and the daily habit advice all come from Anki use. Applying those points to AI flashcards generated from your own notes is commentary written for this comparison, and so is every statement about Fluxo, which rests only on Fluxo's documented features.

Anki is a manual flashcard app with powerful scheduling and a steep learning curve, so its lessons transfer most cleanly to tools where you write cards yourself. Generated cards change one step of that process. The AI section below tests each claim again instead of assuming it carries over.

## What premade decks actually give you

Premade Anki decks lean toward memorization and fact-based cards rather than cards that build connections between ideas. A card that asks you to recall a term from its definition tests one fact. A card that asks how two mechanisms interact makes you build a link between them, and that link is much harder to form from a deck you did not write.

Premade decks also give every topic the same weight. A deck built to span a whole subject spends as much review time on material you already know as on material you don't, so you burn minutes on cards you would pass without thinking. A custom deck avoids that waste because you leave out what you have mastered.

Pure-memorization subjects such as pharmacology are the exception. A premade deck can be acceptable there, provided it forces you to make connections, for example by asking how a drug class relates to the process it affects instead of only asking for its name. **The trade only pays off when the deck still makes you think.**

## Why deck success stories can mislead you

A typical success story goes like this: a student used a particular deck, passed the exam, and now credits the deck. That story proves nothing about cause, because correlation isn't causation and the same student might have passed with a completely different method.

Silent evidence bias makes the picture worse. When you judge a resource only by the people who stuck with it and did well, you never hear from the users who tried it, did poorly, and quit. The success stories you can see are a filtered sample, and the filter removes exactly the outcomes you most need to see.

Before you adopt a deck on the strength of testimonials, ask whether anyone reporting results also reported dropping out. If the only evidence is glowing accounts from people still using it, the deck's real track record is unknown.

## Why making your own cards teaches more

Making your own cards produces better learning because assembling the information does much of the learning work. Writing your own notes beats reading someone else's for the same reason: a finished set of notes lets you skip the decisions that make material stick.

### Card-making time doubles as study time

Most of the time you spend making cards is really time spent learning the material well enough to make good cards. To write one clear card, you first decide what the idea is, how it connects to what you already know, and what a correct answer looks like. A premade card makes all three decisions for you, so the only thing left to practice is recall.

Skip that assembly step and your reviews end up testing your memory of someone else's wording. That kind of review feels easier than recall you built yourself, and the gap shows up when the exam asks the question in a new form.

### Custom cards target your weakest material

Custom cards let you aim at your biggest weaknesses. Premade decks cover everything equally and overemphasize what you already know, so their review time spreads thin across material you have mastered. If you keep mixing up two concepts, you can write three cards on the contrast and skip the topics you handle without effort.

## Where AI flashcards fit when you keep your own notes

AI flashcards generated from your own notes stay tied to what you wrote and need to learn. The cards carry your wording, your examples, and your gaps instead of a generic syllabus, which makes them the closest match to the custom-card advantage above.

The catch is that the cards can only be as targeted as the notes behind them. Thin notes produce thin cards. A note that lists facts without explaining how they relate will generate fact-based cards, which is the same weakness that drags down premade decks.

You keep the learning benefit by reviewing and editing what the generator produces. Read each card, rewrite any that test trivia, and delete duplicates. That editing pass is where you do the assembly work the generator would otherwise do for you.

## Making either option work: daily habits and long-term review

### Why missed days break Anki routines

Skipping Anki days is a common mistake, and it usually happens after a long week or an exam, when motivation is lowest. One missed day lets reviews pile up. A large pile of due cards is harder to start than a small one, so a single lazy day easily turns into a lazy week.

### Why a no-exceptions rule beats case-by-case decisions

Committing to cards every day with no exceptions is easier than deciding case by case whether today counts as an exception. Each exception forces a fresh judgment call at the moment motivation is weakest. With a fixed rule, there is nothing to decide.

### Studying ahead before an important event

Before an important event, study ahead by doing cards in advance so only a small number remain on the day itself. The setup takes three steps:

1. Count the cards due in the days before the event.
2. Work through those cards early so the backlog shrinks each day.
3. On the day itself, review only the small remainder.

### Compounding retention over the long run

Anki's benefit compounds. With a low daily forgetting rate, the theoretical maximum of accumulated knowledge is very large, so the longer you keep going, the more you retain. Each day's reviews protect material built on earlier days, which is why a routine kept for years beats one kept for weeks.
