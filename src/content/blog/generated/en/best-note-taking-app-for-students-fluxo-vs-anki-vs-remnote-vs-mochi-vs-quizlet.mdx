The best note-taking app for students is the one that puts the least work between writing your notes and remembering them. Your notes are the raw material for every study session, and the app you pick decides how much stands between writing them and recalling them. Some apps make you build every flashcard by hand. Others generate study material automatically, but only if you write inside their format. A few offer serious scheduling that scares off anyone new to the idea.

This comparison covers five apps students actually use: Anki, RemNote, Mochi, Quizlet, and Fluxo. Each answers the same question a different way: how do you turn notes into knowledge that stays put?

## How we evaluated these apps

Four things separated these apps: how fast you can capture a note, which study methods the app supports, whether beginners and power users both fit, and whether your data stays yours. We ran each one through a normal student loop: write notes in class or from a reading, turn them into study material, then review over the following weeks.

We favored apps built around notes you write, not around pre-made content or social feeds, and we checked whether you can export and leave. All five target students or fit study work closely, and none pretend to be a research system for professionals. That keeps the choice concrete: you are picking among study tools, not general apps with a study mode bolted on.



## Anki: powerful scheduling with high setup friction

Anki is the oldest and most powerful flashcard app here. Its spaced-repetition algorithm (SM-2) has been refined against decades of memory research, and for students who learn the interface, nothing schedules reviews better.

The cost is a steep learning curve. You build every card by hand, either typing into Anki or importing a CSV, TSV, or semicolon-delimited file. Hundreds of settings sit behind that: card templates, deck structure, timing, review intervals, and the granular control that thrills power users is the same thing that freezes beginners.

That trade-off cuts against most students. With 500 cards to review, Anki's scheduling keeps you efficient and honest. Starting from a handful of cards, still unsure the app is worth it, the setup feels like overkill. Anki never closes the distance between having notes and having flashcards, so you build that bridge yourself.

## RemNote: notes and flashcards in one tool

RemNote is a note-taking app that doubles as a flashcard tool. You write in RemNote's format, mark sections as flashcards through its bidirectional-link system, and the app turns those marked sections into review items automatically.

Students who want one tool tend to like this. Everything lives in one place, and cards come out of your notes without retyping anything. The interface supports nested notes and links, so related ideas connect the way you actually think about them.

The catch is lock-in. The flashcard integration only works cleanly inside RemNote's format, and while export runs, the full bidirectional structure does not survive the move to another app. If RemNote closes or you migrate later, your notes come with you; the connections between them mostly do not.

## Mochi: markdown flashcards with intentional design

Mochi keeps flashcard design deliberately small. You write cards in Markdown, so your study material stays plain text and stays portable, and the app puts its weight behind visual clarity and a review screen with nothing to distract you.

It fits students who like markdown and want a clean, quiet place to review. Mochi does not try to run your whole workflow. You keep notes elsewhere, in Obsidian or Apple Notes, and use Mochi only for spaced repetition. That split appeals to anyone who already has a note system and wants study material kept separate.

The limit is that Mochi generates nothing for you. You write the markdown by hand, which beats Anki for a fluent markdown user but still takes real time. It is also less known than Anki or Quizlet, so shared card sets and community help are thin.

## Quizlet: mass-market study games and ready-made sets

Quizlet is the easiest place for a new student to start. It hosts millions of pre-made sets across nearly every subject, so you search your class, open a set someone already built, and review in seconds. The review comes wrapped in games, from matching to writing modes, that feel more like play than study.

That is both the appeal and the ceiling. When a set already exists for your topic, nothing gets you reviewing faster. Study something niche or freshly assigned and you are back to building cards by hand, one typed or pasted entry at a time.

Quizlet trades depth for breadth and engagement. The games work for short-term recall and for making a session less of a slog. Long-term mastery leans on spaced repetition more than on games, and Quizlet's scheduling is weaker than Anki's or even RemNote's.

## Fluxo: AI-generated flashcards from your own notes

Fluxo starts from the opposite end. You write your notes inside Fluxo, organized with spaces and topics, and its AI generates flashcards, quizzes, and summaries straight from what you wrote. You do the thinking and writing; the app works to make it stick through spaced-repetition review.

That removes the manual flashcard bottleneck. In Anki, RemNote, Mochi, or Quizlet, you write notes somewhere and then face the separate job of turning them into study material. Even where that conversion is automated, as in RemNote, you still have to write in a format the app can parse.

Fluxo drops that step. Your notes are rich-text entries in the app itself, held together by spaces and topics. The AI reads them, picks out the key concepts, and builds flashcards aimed at what matters, while quizzes and summaries come alongside to remix the same notes into different angles for recall.

Review adds a companion mascot, streaks, and gamified sessions that take some of the grind out of repetition. Fluxo also suggests new topics based on your notes, surfacing connections you might have walked past.

## Where Fluxo falls short

Fluxo is not right for every student, and hiding that would waste your time. It has no library of pre-made sets, so if you want to learn fast from someone else's cards, Quizlet's millions of shared sets get you there quicker. Fluxo assumes you write your own notes, which is the strongest way to learn but asks more of you upfront.

It also does not write your notes for you. The AI generates flashcards, quizzes, and summaries from notes you have already written; it does not draft them from scratch. You still have to engage the textbook, lecture, or article directly. That is great for deep learning but a real friction point if you wanted the AI to carry the load.

Data portability is the murkier part. We could not confirm how easily you can export your notes and study material if you switch apps later, and lock-in undercuts the point of a tool you plan to use for years.

Fluxo also skips classroom and teacher management. If you need to run study groups or share notes with classmates in an organized way, this is a personal-use app only.

## Which app fits your study style

Pick Anki if your study needs are complex and you will spend the hours to learn its configuration. Nothing beats it for serious students grinding technical material over the long haul. The curve is steep and the payoff is real.

Pick RemNote if you want notes and flashcards tightly woven and you are fine living inside its ecosystem. It rewards students who build their own knowledge systems and want cards to grow out of their notes.

Pick Mochi if you already write notes in markdown elsewhere and want a clean, distraction-free flashcard app to pair with them. Pick Quizlet if you want to start today with pre-made sets, or you like games and social features, and you study mainstream topics where shared sets already exist.

Pick Fluxo if you write detailed notes and want an AI to pull out and drill the key concepts for you. It is strongest for students who learn by writing and reviewing material they already understand, and who would rather have study material generated from that writing than build it card by card.

## Make your own notes do the studying

Right now the gap is the grind between writing good notes and having something to review them with. You finish a chapter, then face an hour of retyping the important parts into cards before a single review starts. That tax is exactly why so many students write solid notes and then never study from them.

Picture that hour gone. You finish the notes, and the flashcards, quizzes, and summaries are already waiting, scheduled to resurface right when you are about to forget them. That is what Fluxo does: you write rich-text notes inside spaces and topics, its AI turns them into flashcards, quizzes, and summaries, and it spaces the reviews out over time, with a companion mascot and streaks to keep sessions from going stale.

Fluxo will not hand you ready-made decks or write your notes for you: you bring the notes, and it makes them stick.
