## Where these flashcard rules come from

Good flashcards run on a handful of principles that hold up across decades of memory research and millions of study sessions. The short version: keep one deck per major exam instead of a pile of weekly decks, let spaced repetition resurface material over time, put one fact on each card, use images to recruit visual memory, and learn the concept before you try to memorize it. These stack. Single-fact cards make spaced repetition scheduling work properly, and understanding first makes both worth doing.

## Understand before you memorize

Memorizing facts you don't understand gives you recall that collapses under exam pressure. Unconnected facts are hard to retrieve and harder to apply to a question you haven't seen before. Read the textbook, sit through the lecture, work the problems, and only reach for flashcards once you can explain why something is true. The cards then reinforce a structure that already exists in your head.

The Pareto principle applies to study planning as much as anything else: chase the high-yield foundations before the low-yield details. Major bone landmarks in anatomy earn their place on a card; every minor articulation does not. Same in pharmacology, where drug mechanisms outrank the side effects of rare variants. Aim your cards at the 20 percent of material that shows up on 80 percent of the exam.

## Consolidate into one deck per exam

Splitting flashcards by week, class, or topic feels organized and quietly wrecks your review. Put everything for one major exam into a single large deck and use tags inside it if you need the structure. Review then cycles through all your material rather than whichever slice you happened to open.

One big deck builds the habit of reviewing everything, which is exactly what a cumulative exam rewards. On the MCAT, USMLE Step 1, or COMLEX, what you learned in month one is fair game in month six. Spaced repetition handles the load problem for you: intervals stretch out for material you've mastered, so month-one cards cost only minutes a day by the time month three arrives. New content keeps arriving and the old content keeps costing less.

## Apply the minimum information principle

A card with several facts on it breaks your schedule in a specific way. The hardest sub-item gets forgotten again and again, and every miss resets the whole card's interval, so you end up drilling the three things you already know to fix the one you don't. Partial recall makes it worse: getting three of four sub-items right can pass as a success while the gap stays.

Split those cards. "Name the three skin layers and their primary function" becomes three cards, one each for epidermis, dermis, and hypodermis with its function. Each schedules on its own, so forgetting the hypodermis card leaves the other two alone at their long intervals.

### Use cloze deletions for speed

Cloze deletion cards are fill-in-the-blank. Rather than "What is the mitochondrion's function?", you write "The [[mitochondrion]] is the powerhouse of the cell" and recall the covered term during review.

The format enforces single-fact cards by construction, since each blank holds one concept and stuffing several ideas into a blank stops making sense. Cloze cards are also fast to build: paste text from a slide or your notes, bracket the key terms, and you're done. There's no rewriting each statement into a question.

Speed is the reason this matters. Most people who abandon a flashcard system abandon it because card-writing became a second homework assignment on top of the first, and recycling notes you already took removes that tax.

### Add images to reinforce memory

Visual information sticks better than text alone, which pays off most in anatomy, chemistry, or geography where spatial relationships carry the meaning. A Krebs cycle diagram makes a card about the Krebs cycle stronger. So does a photograph of muscle attachments on an anatomy card.

The image doesn't even have to depict the card's content. A photo of the researcher associated with a disease, a shot of the lab where the discovery happened, or a diagram of adjacent material all work as retrieval hooks, because any visual you associate with the fact gives recall something to grab.

Anki's Image Occlusion Enhanced plugin takes this further by letting you hide parts of a diagram so the diagram tests you. On a Krebs cycle, occlude each enzyme name, label, and intermediate separately. Every occlusion becomes its own card, which splits the material along minimum-information lines without you thinking about it.

## Test your cards, then fix the bad ones

Sample your cards before you commit to reviewing them for months. Can you answer quickly, without hesitating? Does the card test one fact or four? Is the wording ambiguous enough that a correct answer might read as wrong? A card you've missed consistently for two weeks is usually a drafting problem, not a memory problem: too vague, too many concepts, or missing the context that makes the answer findable. Delete it, rewrite it, or split it.

What you're building is a system you'll actually keep using. A hundred clean cards reviewed every day beat a thousand sloppy ones you avoid opening. Start small, check that each card is unambiguous, and scale from there.

## Writing cards in minutes instead of evenings

The principles above are simple to state and tedious to execute by hand. Splitting one dense lecture slide into eight single-fact cards, bracketing the cloze blanks, hunting down a usable diagram, tagging everything so it lands in the right deck: that's the work that turns a 30-minute study block into a two-hour formatting session, and it's why most decks die in week three.
