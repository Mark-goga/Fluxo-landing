Finishing an online course feels productive, and a week later most of it has evaporated. The honest answer to how to remember what you learn from online courses is unglamorous: memory responds to what you pull out of your head, not to what you pour into it. Rewatching a module is the most popular fix and the one that reliably fails.

## Where This Advice Comes From

These habits come from people who publish what they learn on a schedule. Ali Abdaal, a medical student turned educator, has written a weekly newsletter called Sunday Snippets since 2018, and every issue forces him to retrieve the week's learning, organize it, and put it in words someone else can follow. His team also runs a monthly Level Up Loom, where each member records a short presentation on something they picked up recently. The visible output is a newsletter or a video; the useful output is a memory that survives.

## Survey the Whole Map Before You Dive In

Most learners open lesson one and start grinding. That builds blind spots: you go deep on one area without knowing which neighboring areas exist or how they connect to the thing you actually want to do. Surveying the whole field first gives you a map, so each new piece of material lands somewhere instead of floating loose. An hour on the map before 20 hours on one topic keeps you from mastering a corner that does nothing for your goal.

## Learn Just-in-Time, Not Just-in-Case

Just-in-time learning means picking something up at the moment you need it, usually because a problem in front of you demands it. Just-in-case learning means stockpiling material on the theory that it might matter someday. For retention the first beats the second by a wide margin, because your brain files the idea next to the situation that required it. Timing does more work here than the number of repetitions.

### Follow Your Curiosity as a Compass

Curiosity predicts what stays. In one study, participants who rated themselves more curious about a fact were far more likely to recall it later than facts they felt neutral about. That makes curiosity a usable compass instead of a distraction: learn the thing while the itch is live, because retention peaks there. Grinding through material you feel indifferent about produces weaker memories even when you put in more hours.

## Try It Before You Study the Theory

Reading the theory and then applying it feels like the responsible order. Flip it. Attempt the thing first, struggle with it a little, then read the explanation, and the explanation sticks because you have somewhere to put it. A tutorial that walks you through working code before naming the pattern works for this reason: you have already met the problem the pattern solves.

## Teach What You Learn to Lock It In

Explaining something to another person makes you retrieve it, structure it, and phrase it so it lands, which is three kinds of reinforcement in one pass. Psychologists call this the Protégé effect: teaching or explaining something strengthens your own memory of it. Writing up a lesson, recording yourself talking through a concept, or walking a colleague through it all beat another silent pass over your notes. Your audience can be one mildly interested person and the effect still holds.

## Test Yourself Instead of Rereading

Rereading is the most popular study method and one of the weakest. Active recall, meaning you test yourself on the material without looking at it, strengthens memory far more than rereading notes, highlighting, or clicking through the slides again. Studies on exam preparation land in the same place: read the material once, then quiz yourself, and your recall beats that of someone who read it several times over. Most people reread anyway, since a quiz surfaces the gaps and rereading lets you avoid them.

### Why Retrieval Beats Cramming

<PullQuote text="Memory retention comes from retrieving information out of your brain, not from putting more in." />

Cramming feels like learning because everything is still sitting in short-term memory the next morning. Durable memory comes from retrieval: each time you pull a fact out by testing, teaching, or explaining it, you make it easier to pull out again. Spreading those retrievals across days and weeks compounds the effect, which is why several short sessions over a month beat one long night before the exam.
